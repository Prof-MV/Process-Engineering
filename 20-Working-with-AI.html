9  Working Effectively with AI

9.1 Learning Objectives

After completing this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Understand AI’s role in modern engineering work and its limitations
  2. Apply the four steps of effective AI use: Define, Prompt, Iterate, Validate
  3. Apply prompt engineering principles to get better AI responses
  4. Distinguish between poor, adequate, and excellent prompts
  5. Select appropriate AI tools for different tasks (chat interfaces and projects, NotebookLM, coding agents, browser extensions)
  6. Use iterative AI collaboration to solve complex engineering problems
  7. Critically evaluate AI-generated content for accuracy and safety
  8. Identify tasks suited for AI assistance versus human expertise
  9. Apply ethical considerations when using AI in engineering contexts
  10. Distinguish chat, projects, tool use, agents and multi-agent systems, and set the right permissions and review for each
  11. Protect confidential information, and document and disclose your use of AI

9.2 Introduction: AI in the Modern Workplace

Artificial Intelligence has fundamentally changed how engineering work is performed. According to research, AI is predominantly used for coding and technical work (34% of professional use), with complex tasks showing potential 12x speedups compared to traditional methods.

However, there’s a critical paradox: more complex tasks have lower success rates (61-66% versus 70% for simpler tasks). This means engineers must develop skills to effectively collaborate with AI, not just delegate to it.

9.2.1 The Education Paradox

Key Insight: There is a nearly perfect correlation between prompt sophistication and response quality. You need strong foundational knowledge to get quality AI assistance. “AI will do it for me” is a trap.

Discussion: Why does expertise improve AI results?

The “education paradox” reveals a fundamental truth about AI collaboration:

  • Better questions lead to better answers: Experts know what to ask and how to frame problems
  • Domain knowledge enables validation: You can only verify AI output if you understand the domain
  • Iterative refinement requires expertise: Knowing when to push back or ask follow-up questions
  • Critical evaluation is essential: AI can sound confident while being wrong

Think about it: If you don’t understand the fundamentals of motor selection, how would you know if an AI recommendation is correct or dangerously undersized?

Which statement is closest to how you use AI tools today? (No right answer, show of hands.)

  • A. I do not use them
  • B. I ask them questions and copy useful parts
  • C. I use them to write or fix code
  • D. I give them multi-step jobs and review the result
Reveal after the vote

No wrong answer. Keep your answer in mind. By the end of the chapter you will be able to say which of these you do well, which you do carelessly, and what you would change.

Think – pair – share (3 min). The education paradox says your expertise decides how much help AI gives you. Describe one topic from your program where you could judge an AI answer well, and one where you could not. What would you do about the second?

9.2.2 AI Impact by Industry

AI Impact Across Engineering Industries
Industry Common AI Applications Potential Speedup Success Rate Human Oversight Needed
Automotive Simulation modeling, control systems, predictive maintenance 10-12x 62% Critical
Food Processing Process optimization, quality control, equipment diagnostics 8-10x 68% High
Defense System design, modeling, compliance documentation 10-12x 61% Critical
Mining Equipment diagnostics, predictive maintenance, safety analysis 8-10x 65% High
General Manufacturing Documentation, troubleshooting guides, process analysis 6-8x 70% Moderate
Why do automotive and defense require “Critical” human oversight?

Safety-critical applications require mandatory human validation because:

  1. Regulatory requirements: ISO 26262 (automotive) and MIL-STDs (defense) require documented human review
  2. Liability: Engineers are legally responsible for designs, not AI tools
  3. Failure consequences: Errors can cause injury, death, or mission failure
  4. AI limitations: Current AI cannot reliably reason about edge cases and failure modes
  5. Standards compliance: AI cannot certify compliance with safety standards

Best practice: Use AI for initial research and drafting, but always validate with human experts and formal analysis methods (FMEA, FTA, HAZOP).

9.3 Prompt Engineering Fundamentals

Prompt engineering is the skill of crafting inputs to AI systems that produce useful, accurate, and relevant outputs. In engineering contexts, this skill is becoming as important as technical writing.

Before you read: What makes a good question?

Think about the difference between these two questions:

  1. “Can you help me with my motor problem?”
  2. “I have a 3-phase 460V 50HP motor that trips its overload after 15 minutes of running. Ambient temperature is 35°C. What diagnostic steps should I follow?”

Which question would get better help from a human expert? The same principle applies to AI. Before reading about prompt engineering, consider what information you would need to give a colleague to get useful help.

9.3.1 The Seven Rules of Effective Prompts

How the seven rules relate to R-C-T-C-F. In Chapter 10 you will use a shorter, five-letter checklist for prompts, R-C-T-C-F (Role, Context, Task, Constraints, Format). It is the compact form of the seven rules:

R-C-T-C-F Comes from rule
Role 2. Use role-playing
Context 1. Be specific and structured
Task 3. Break down complex tasks
Constraints 6. Avoid ambiguity, 7. Add ethical guardrails
Format 1. Be specific and structured, 4. Include examples

Rule 5 (request reasoning) is a technique you add to any prompt rather than a part of its structure.

9.4 Prompt Quality Comparison: Bad, Medium, and Good

The following examples demonstrate the dramatic difference that prompt quality makes. Each example shows the same task approached three ways.

9.4.1 Example 1: Motor Selection

Motor Selection Prompt Comparison
Quality Level Prompt Expected AI Response
Bad Help me pick a motor. Generic motor types list with no specific guidance. AI cannot determine application, size, or requirements.
Medium I need to select a motor for a conveyor system. The conveyor moves boxes that weigh up to 50kg. What motor should I use? Basic motor power estimate, general motor types suggested, but missing: duty cycle, environment, power supply, efficiency requirements, safety factors.
Good Act as a senior electromechanical engineer. I need to select an AC induction motor for a conveyor belt system with the following specifications:
- Load: cardboard boxes up to 50kg each
- Belt speed: 0.5 m/s
- Belt length: 10m, width: 0.6m
- Incline: 5 degrees
- Operating environment: food processing plant (IP65 required)
- Power supply: 480V 3-phase

Please provide:
1. Required torque calculation
2. Motor power recommendation with 25% safety factor
3. Recommended motor specifications (frame size, efficiency class)
4. Three specific motor models from major manufacturers
5. Mounting considerations

Format as a technical memo with calculations shown.		 Complete technical analysis with calculated torque (~85 Nm), motor sizing (1.5-2.2 kW), specific model recommendations (e.g., ABB M3BP, Siemens 1LE1), IP65-rated food-safe options, and mounting specifications.

9.4.2 Example 2: Troubleshooting a PLC System

PLC Troubleshooting Prompt Comparison
Quality Level Prompt Why This Works (or Doesn't)
Bad My PLC isn't working. Fix it. No context: What PLC? What's the symptom? What have you tried? AI will give generic advice that wastes time.
Medium I have an Allen-Bradley PLC that's showing a fault. The output for a motor starter isn't turning on even though the program shows it should be. What could be wrong? Better: Identifies equipment and basic symptom. Missing: specific I/O addresses, what's been checked, system configuration, urgency.
Good Act as an experienced automation technician troubleshooting a control system issue.

System: Allen-Bradley CompactLogix L33ER
Problem: Output O:2/5 (motor starter for pump P-101) fails to energize

Observations:
- Rung logic in RSLogix shows OTE instruction is TRUE
- Output status LED on I/O module is OFF
- Motor starter coil tests good (24VDC, 85 ohms)
- Other outputs on same module work correctly
- Problem started after yesterday's power outage

Please provide:
1. Systematic troubleshooting steps in order of likelihood
2. How to test each potential failure point
3. Safety precautions for each test
4. Possible root causes ranked by probability
5. Preventive measures to avoid recurrence

Format as a troubleshooting checklist I can use in the field.		 Excellent: Provides complete context, specific observations, clear deliverables, and practical output format. Enables targeted, actionable response.

9.4.3 Example 3: Safety Analysis

Safety Analysis Prompt Comparison - Critical Application
Quality Level Prompt Risk Assessment
Bad Is this machine safe? DANGEROUS: Vague prompts about safety can lead to incomplete analysis and missed hazards. Never acceptable for safety-critical work.
Medium I have a robotic welding cell. What safety features does it need? INADEQUATE: Will get generic safety device list but miss application-specific hazards, risk levels, and compliance requirements.
Good Act as a certified machine safety engineer (TUV or equivalent).

I need to perform a risk assessment for a new robotic welding cell with the following configuration:
- Robot: FANUC ArcMate 100iD, 12kg payload
- Process: MIG welding, automotive brackets
- Cell size: 4m x 4m
- Operator tasks: Load/unload parts every 45 seconds
- Adjacent to manual assembly line (2m clearance)

Please provide:
1. Hazard identification using ISO 12100 categories
2. Risk assessment matrix for top 5 hazards
3. Required safeguarding per OSHA and ISO 10218-2
4. Recommended safety system architecture
5. Validation testing requirements

Reference applicable standards (ISO, ANSI/RIA, CSA) for each recommendation.		 APPROPRIATE: Enables comprehensive, standards-based response. Still requires human expert validation before implementation.

Warning: For safety-critical applications, AI should NEVER be the final authority. Always validate AI-generated safety analyses with certified professionals and applicable standards.

9.7 The Four Steps of Effective AI Use

The research shows that effectively working with AI follows a consistent four-step process. Master these steps to maximize the value of AI collaboration.

The Four Steps of Effective AI Collaboration
A systematic approach to getting maximum value from AI tools
1. DEFINE the Task
Break complex problems into AI-manageable tasks. Identify what you need and what AI can help with.
→ Task decomposition
2. PROMPT with Context
Write structured prompts using the seven rules. Include role, context, constraints, and deliverables.
→ Prompt engineering
3. ITERATE and Refine
Review AI output critically. Ask follow-up questions. Refine through multiple conversation turns.
→ Multi-turn dialogue
4. VALIDATE and Apply
Verify accuracy against standards and expertise. Apply human judgment. Integrate into final work.
→ Critical evaluation

9.7.1 Step 1: Define the Task

Before engaging with AI, clearly define what you need:

  • What is the end goal? A calculation, a design, troubleshooting guidance?
  • What constraints apply? Budget, standards, materials, timeline?
  • What do you already know? Your expertise shapes the conversation
  • Can AI actually help with this? Some tasks require physical inspection or human judgment

Key Insight: The better you understand the problem, the better AI can help. Vague understanding leads to vague prompts and useless responses.

Think – pair – share (3 min). A supervisor says, “Make our packaging line faster.” Before touching any AI tool, write down three questions you need answered to define the task. Which of them could you answer yourself, and which need data or people?

9.7.2 Step 2: Prompt with Context

Apply the seven rules of effective prompting:

  1. Assign a role to establish expertise level
  2. Provide complete context and background
  3. Specify constraints and requirements
  4. List clear deliverables
  5. Request step-by-step reasoning
  6. Define the output format
  7. Include relevant standards

Two additions for current tools:

  • State what “done” looks like. “A table of every mismatch with drawing and BOM line numbers” gives the AI a target and gives you a way to check it.
  • Put standing context in a project. Role, units, standards and templates that you would otherwise repeat in every prompt belong in project instructions (see Section 9.8.1).

9.7.3 Step 3: Iterate and Refine

Rarely is the first response perfect. Plan for 3-6 turns of conversation:

  • Review critically: What’s useful? What’s missing? What’s wrong?
  • Ask targeted follow-up questions
  • Request expansions on specific areas
  • Challenge assumptions and ask for alternatives
  • Start a fresh conversation when the topic changes or the thread drifts, and carry over a short summary (see Section 9.8.2)

9.7.4 Step 4: Validate and Apply

AI output is a starting point, not a final answer:

  • Verify calculations and units
  • Cross-check against standards and codes
  • Apply your engineering judgment
  • Document what came from AI assistance
  • Ask the AI to run the calculation in code and show the code, then check that the formula and units are right
  • Get a second opinion: a fresh chat or another tool asked to find the errors, not to confirm the answer
  • Take responsibility for the final work product
Case Study: When validation caught an AI error

Scenario: An engineering student used AI to calculate the required conveyor belt width for a food processing line handling 500 kg/hr of bulk product.

AI Response: Recommended a 300mm belt based on throughput calculations.

Validation Issue: The AI correctly calculated volumetric flow but failed to consider: - Minimum belt width for the discharge chute (400mm required) - Product spread factor for irregular materials - Safety factor for surge capacity

Actual Requirement: 500mm belt per facility standards.

Lesson: AI calculations may be mathematically correct but miss practical engineering constraints. Always validate against physical requirements, codes, and site-specific standards.

When AI is the wrong tool altogether is covered in When NOT to Use AI later in this chapter.

9.8 AI Tools and Their Use Cases

Different AI tools serve different purposes. Understanding when to use each tool maximizes productivity.

9.8.1 From Chatbot to Agent: Five Levels of AI Tools

The language model is only the core of what you use today. Most current products wrap it in layers that change how you work with it. Each layer adds capability, and each adds a new way to go wrong.

Level What it is Engineering example Main new risk
1. Chat You type, the model replies. It knows only what is in the conversation Explain what a thermal overload relay does Hallucination, no access to your documents
2. Projects A saved workspace with standing instructions (your role, units, standards, format) and reference files that every chat in it starts with One project per product line, holding the customer specification and your PFMEA template Stale or confidential files left in the project
3. Tools and connectors The model can search, run code, read your files or query company systems (often through the Model Context Protocol, MCP, a common plug-in standard) Check a Cpk calculation by running it instead of guessing It can now act, not just talk
4. Agents The model works in a loop: plan, call a tool, read the result, decide the next step, until the goal is met “Reconcile this BOM against the drawing and list every mismatch” Mistakes compound over many steps unnoticed
5. Multi-agent systems (“swarms”) An orchestrator splits a large job and hands the parts to sub-agents, each with its own fresh context, often running in parallel. A reviewer agent can check the others Survey 40 work instructions against a revised safety standard Cost, and more places for errors to hide

An agent is therefore a language model plus tools plus a loop. The same model that hallucinated a standard number in a chat can, as an agent, look the standard up, read it and quote it. That is a real improvement, and it is also why agents are used for tasks like writing and running the Python code in this course.

Best practices for working with agents and projects:

  • State the goal and the definition of done. “Every mismatch listed with its drawing and BOM line number” beats “check the BOM”.
  • Put standing rules in project instructions, not in every prompt: units, applicable standards, naming conventions, what the agent must never do.
  • Plan first, then execute. Ask for the plan and approve it before the agent changes anything. It is cheap to correct a plan and expensive to correct a finished job.
  • Grant the least permission that works. Start read-only. Require your approval before it writes files, sends messages or touches any PLC, ERP or live production system. Never let an agent modify safety-rated logic.
  • Make it verify its own work. Ask for calculations to be run in code, tests to be written and run, and every claim tied to a source document. Then have a separate reviewer (a second agent or a fresh chat) check the result, because a model is a poor judge of its own output.
  • Treat everything the agent reads as untrusted. Text inside a web page, PDF or email can contain hidden instructions that an agent may follow (prompt injection). Do not let an agent that reads outside material also hold access to confidential files or send data out.
  • Use multiple agents only when the job is large and parallel. For one focused task a single agent is cheaper, faster and easier to check.
  • Keep a human accountable. Review the agent’s log of what it did, not just its final answer, and sign off as you would for a junior engineer’s work.

Group Discussion — pick the setup. For each task choose chat, project, single agent or multi-agent, and justify your choice: (1) explaining what a thermal overload relay does; (2) drafting weekly supplier-quality reports in your company’s template; (3) checking 40 work instructions for conflicts with a revised safety standard; (4) changing the ladder logic on a running guarding interlock.

Suggested answers: (1) chat; (2) project, because the template and rules repeat; (3) multi-agent, because the job is large and parallel, with a human reviewing every conflict it flags; (4) no AI acting alone. AI may help you review the logic, but a qualified person makes and validates the change.

Your supervisor asks you to set up an AI agent to tidy the maintenance work-order backlog. What access do you give it on day one?

  • A. Read-only access to a copy of the work-order export
  • B. Read and write access to the live CMMS
  • C. Full access to the CMMS, email and MES so it can “close the loop”
  • D. Write access to the PLC so it can stop machines with open orders
Reveal after the vote

A. Start read-only, on a copy, and review the plan and a sample of its output. Widen access one step at a time as trust is earned. Option D is never acceptable. Option C also removes the barrier that stops a prompt-injected agent from leaking data.

9.8.2 Managing Context: Context Engineering

The seven rules structure one message. With projects and agents, what matters more is the whole context the model works from. Four habits cover most of it:

  1. Standing context goes in the project. Role, units, standards, templates and formats belong in project instructions and reference files, so each message can be short.
  2. Give the right documents, not all of them. Attach the relevant specification and drawing, not the whole shared drive. Irrelevant material dilutes the answer.
  3. Show an example of the output you want. One good sample report or table is worth a paragraph of description.
  4. Start fresh when the topic changes. A long conversation full of old tangents is polluted context. Open a new chat, and carry over only a short summary.

9.8.3 Chat Interfaces (Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini)

Web-based conversational AI platforms are the most versatile starting point for AI collaboration.

Common Engineering Use Cases for Chat Interfaces
Use Case Description Example Prompt
Technical Explanations Explain complex concepts, standards, or technical principles in accessible terms 'Explain the difference between ASIL levels in ISO 26262 for automotive safety'
Document Drafting Draft reports, specifications, procedures, or technical memos 'Draft a preventive maintenance procedure for a hydraulic press'
Calculation Verification Double-check hand calculations; request step-by-step solutions 'Verify my torque calculation: 50kg load, 0.3m arm, need Nm result'
Brainstorming Solutions Generate alternative approaches to engineering problems 'What are three approaches to detect bearing wear before failure?'
Code Generation Write PLC ladder logic concepts, Python scripts, or MATLAB code 'Write Python code to calculate conveyor belt speed from encoder pulses'
Standard Interpretation Interpret requirements from ISO, OSHA, NEC, or other standards 'What does NFPA 70E require for arc flash labeling on electrical panels?'

Best Practices for Chat Interfaces:

  • Start new conversations for new topics (avoid context pollution)
  • Use the “regenerate” feature to see alternative responses
  • Upload images of diagrams, schematics, or equipment for visual analysis
  • Copy important responses to notes (conversations can be lost)
  • Use projects for recurring work, so standing instructions and reference files travel with every chat
  • Switch on web search for anything current, and ask for sources you can open
  • Use the thinking mode for multi-step problems and the fast mode for drafting and simple questions
  • Ask for calculations to be run in code and shown, instead of worked out in prose

9.8.4 NotebookLM (Google)

NotebookLM is specifically designed for source-grounded research. Upload documents, and the AI answers questions based only on your sources.

NotebookLM Features for Engineering Work
Feature Description Engineering Application
Source-Based Answers Answers come directly from your uploaded documents, reducing hallucination Upload equipment manuals and ask specific troubleshooting questions
Citation Links Every answer includes clickable citations to the exact source passage Verify AI claims against original standards documents
Audio Overview Generates AI podcast-style audio summaries of your documents for learning Listen to safety standard summaries during commute or shop floor time
Multi-Document Synthesis Combines information from multiple PDFs, websites, or text files Compare multiple vendor datasheets or competing standards

Example NotebookLM Workflow:

  1. Upload: ISO 12100, ISO 13849-1, equipment manual PDF
  2. Ask: “What risk assessment steps does ISO 12100 require?”
  3. Review: AI provides answer with direct citations to document sections
  4. Generate: Create an audio overview to reinforce learning

When to Use NotebookLM: When you need answers grounded in specific documents rather than general AI knowledge. Ideal for standards compliance, equipment documentation, and research synthesis.

9.8.5 Coding Agents (Claude Code and Similar)

Claude Code is a terminal-based AI assistant that can read, write, and execute code in your actual project environment. It is an example of an agent (see above): it plans, edits files, runs commands and checks the results in a loop, asking for your permission along the way.

Claude Code Capabilities for Technical Work
Capability Description Example
File Operations Read, write, and edit files directly in your project Edit src/motor_control.py to add speed ramping
Code Development Generate, refactor, and debug code with full context Write a Python script to parse PLC data logs
Command Execution Run build commands, tests, and system tools Run pytest and fix any failing tests
Project Context Understands your entire codebase structure Find all files that reference the 'Pump' class
Git Integration Create commits, branches, and pull requests Commit changes with descriptive message

Engineering Applications:

  • PLC Code Analysis: Review and understand legacy ladder logic or structured text
  • Data Processing: Create scripts to analyze production data, SPC charts, or sensor logs
  • Documentation Generation: Auto-generate code documentation or API references
  • Test Automation: Write unit tests for engineering calculations

Example Session:

> claude "Analyze the motor_control.py file and suggest improvements for
         handling encoder signal noise"

Claude Code reads the file, understands the context, and provides specific
recommendations with code changes that can be applied directly.

9.8.6 Browser Extensions (Claude Extension, etc.)

Browser extensions provide instant AI assistance without leaving your current workflow.

Browser Extension Use Cases
Use Case How It Helps Example
Webpage Summarization Summarize long technical articles or datasheets instantly On a 20-page motor datasheet: 'What is the rated torque at 1750 RPM?'
Technical Documentation Ask questions about the documentation page you're reading On Allen-Bradley KB: 'How do I configure this fault code?'
Email Composition Draft professional responses to technical inquiries 'Help me respond professionally to this RFQ'

Best Practices:

9.8.7 Working with Images, Files and Data

Current chat tools accept more than text. Three uses matter in engineering:

  • Photos and screenshots: equipment nameplates, wiring diagrams, HMI alarm screens, a damaged part. The AI can read the label, describe the picture and suggest what to check.
  • Documents: datasheets, manuals and specifications as PDFs. Ask it to answer from the document and to quote the passage, so you can find it.
  • Data files: a CSV or spreadsheet of production or sensor data. The best tools write and run code on the file, so the numbers come from a calculation and not from the model’s memory.

The risks are specific. Misread digits on a blurry nameplate (an 8 read as a 3), a number that is plausible but not on the page, and units that are dropped. Always compare the AI’s reading against the original, and never upload customer drawings or plant data to a public tool (Section 9.13.2).

Activity (6 min): nameplate check. Take a photo of the label on your phone charger or laptop power adapter.

  1. Upload it to a chatbot with the prompt: “Read this label. List input voltage, output voltage, current and power, with units.”
  2. Check every value against the label itself. How many did it get right?
  3. Ask: “What is the maximum power this device can deliver, and how did you work it out?” Did it show its arithmetic? Is the arithmetic right?

Report to the class: any misreads, dropped units, or confident wrong answers?

9.9 The Iterative Approach: Multi-Turn AI Collaboration

Complex engineering problems are rarely solved in a single prompt. The iterative approach mirrors how you would work with a human expert.

9.9.1 The Iteration Cycle

The Iterative AI Collaboration Cycle
Professional engineers rarely get perfect results from a single prompt
1. Initial Prompt
Start with your best attempt at a complete prompt
2. Review Response
Read critically: What's useful? What's wrong?
3. Identify Gaps
Note missing info, errors, or areas needing depth
4. Refine & Expand
Ask follow-up questions to fill gaps and correct
5. Validate Results
Cross-check with standards, calculations, and expertise
6. Synthesize Final Output
Combine iterations into final deliverable

In turn 6 of a long chat, the AI starts contradicting a design constraint you gave in turn 2. What is the best move?

  • A. Repeat the constraint in capital letters and keep going
  • B. Start a new chat with a short summary of decisions and constraints, or move the rules into a project
  • C. Tell the AI it is wrong and ask it to apologise
  • D. Accept the new answer because it is the most recent
Reveal after the vote

B. A long thread fills the context window with old tangents, and details get lost. Restating the essentials in a fresh conversation (or in project instructions) restores focus. Shouting does not help, and the most recent answer is not the most correct one.

9.9.2 Worked Example: Designing a Conveyor Control System

Turn 1 - Initial Prompt:

Act as an automation engineer. Design a basic control system for a
conveyor belt in a packaging line. The conveyor is 15m long and
transports boxes to a palletizing station.

AI Response Summary: Generic overview with basic motor control, start/stop logic, and simple sensors.

Turn 2 - Adding Specificity:

Good start. Now expand with these details:
- Add accumulation zones (3 zones, 5m each) with photo-eye sensors
- Include a reject station at the 10m mark for overweight boxes
- Integrate with an upstream case sealer (need handshake signals)
- Use Allen-Bradley CompactLogix as the controller

Show the I/O list and basic ladder logic structure.

AI Response Summary: Detailed I/O list, zone control logic, sensor placement, and communication requirements.

Turn 3 - Safety and Edge Cases:

Now address:
1. What happens if the downstream palletizer faults?
2. Add E-stop integration per ISO 13849 PLd requirements
3. How do we handle box jams in each zone?
4. What's the startup sequence after E-stop reset?

Include safety relay wiring diagram description.

AI Response Summary: Complete safety integration, fault handling, jam detection, and recovery procedures.

Turn 4 - Final Validation:

Review your complete design and:
1. List any assumptions you made that I should verify
2. Identify potential failure modes you haven't addressed
3. What information would you need to finalize this for implementation?
4. Are there any code standards (ISA, IEC) I should reference?

AI Response Summary: Comprehensive assumption list, remaining questions, and standards references for documentation.

Key Point: This four-turn conversation produced a design that would have required a much longer single prompt to achieve, and the iterative approach allowed for course correction along the way.

Try it yourself: Practice iterative prompting

Turn 2: Add specifics (sensor types, communication protocol, integration with existing systems)

Turn 3: Address failure modes and safety (what happens if a sensor fails? How are critical temperature excursions handled?)

Compare your results: Did the iterative approach give you a more complete design than your initial prompt would have?

9.11 Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs

AI can produce confident-sounding responses that are completely wrong. Engineers must develop critical evaluation skills.

9.11.1 Red Flags in AI Responses

Red Flags in AI-Generated Engineering Content
Red Flag Example Your Response
Calculations Without Units "The motor needs 150" - 150 what? HP? kW? Nm? Always request calculations with units shown; verify dimensional analysis
Missing Safety Considerations Design has no E-stop, no guarding discussion, no lockout provisions Explicitly ask for safety analysis; cross-reference with standards
Outdated Standards Referenced References NEC 2014 when 2023 is current; cites withdrawn ISO standards Verify all standard references; check publication dates
Overconfident Language "This will definitely work" or "This is the only solution" Be skeptical; request alternatives and trade-offs
Generic Without Specifics "Use appropriate sensors" without specifying type, range, or model Push for specific part numbers, ranges, and specifications
Ignoring Stated Constraints You specified 24VDC and AI designs for 120VAC Re-state constraints clearly; verify compliance in response

9.11.3 Verification in the Age of Agents

When an agent does ten steps before it answers, checking only the final answer is not enough. Add four habits:

  1. Read the log. Agents show what they searched, which files they changed and which commands they ran. Skim it for steps you did not expect.
  2. Require evidence. Every number gets a source (a page, a file and line, a calculation you can rerun). No source, no trust.
  3. Test, do not trust. Where the agent wrote code or a calculation, run it on a case where you already know the answer.
  4. Use a separate reviewer. A fresh chat (or a different tool) asked to find the errors is more useful than asking the original to double-check itself. Models tend to defend their own work.

A reviewer is still software. For anything safety-related, the reviewer is a qualified person.

Activity (8 min): red-flag hunt. Here is an AI answer to “Size a motor for a 15 m conveyor carrying 500 kg/h.” In pairs, mark every red flag you can find.

“The conveyor needs a 3 motor, and this will definitely work. You can use a standard NEC 2014 enclosure. Connect it to the 120 VAC panel you mentioned. Use appropriate sensors for the jam detection. No further safety features are needed because the belt moves slowly.”

Reveal the flags
  1. “3 motor”: 3 what (hp, kW)? A number without units.
  2. “Definitely work”: overconfident language with no calculation shown.
  3. “NEC 2014”: outdated standard reference (and is NEC even the right code for your site?).
  4. “120 VAC panel you mentioned”: you said 24 VDC. It ignored a stated constraint.
  5. “Appropriate sensors”: generic, with no type, range or model.
  6. “No further safety features”: no E-stop, guarding or lockout discussion at all.

9.12 When NOT to Use AI

AI is a tool, not a replacement for engineering judgment. Recognize its limitations.

9.12.1 AI Excels At:

  • Generating first drafts and starting points
  • Explaining concepts and standards
  • Creating documentation templates
  • Performing routine calculations (with verification)
  • Suggesting troubleshooting approaches
  • Comparing options and trade-offs

9.12.2 AI Struggles With:

Tasks Requiring Human Engineering Judgment
Limitation Description Example
Site-Specific Context AI doesn't know your plant layout, existing equipment, or local practices Recommending motor location without knowing available floor space or cable routes
Current Conditions AI cannot inspect, measure, or observe actual equipment conditions Cannot diagnose bearing wear by sound or vibration feel
Ethical Judgment Decisions balancing safety, cost, and risk require human judgment Choosing between immediate shutdown vs. controlled stop with production impact
Novel Situations Unprecedented problems may not have training data to draw from First-of-kind machine with no similar designs to reference
Physical Intuition Experience-based intuition about what 'feels wrong' in a machine Recognizing that a pump 'doesn't sound right' before instruments show a problem
Accountability AI cannot stamp drawings, sign off on safety, or take legal responsibility A P.Eng. must review and approve critical designs; AI cannot be licensed

9.12.3 Try it yourself: AI or human?

Key insight: AI excels at information synthesis and drafting. Physical observation, legal certification and real-time safety decisions require human expertise.

9.13 Ethical Considerations

Using AI in engineering work carries professional and ethical responsibilities.

9.13.1 Key Principles

  1. Transparency: Disclose AI assistance where required by professional standards or employer policy (see Section 9.13.3)

  2. Verification: Never submit AI-generated work as final without verification by qualified personnel

  3. Accountability: You remain responsible for work product regardless of AI assistance

  4. Confidentiality: Do not input proprietary designs, client data, or trade secrets into public AI systems (see Section 9.13.2)

  5. Competence: AI assistance does not substitute for required qualifications or experience

9.13.2 Confidentiality and Intellectual Property

Warning

Career Risk: Mishandling intellectual property with AI tools is a fireable offence at most manufacturers. Read this section twice. Agents and connectors raise the stakes, because they can read and send more than you type.

The default assumption to adopt: anything you paste into a public LLM (Claude.ai free tier, ChatGPT free tier, Google Gemini, free Copilot) may be:

  • Retained by the provider
  • Used to improve future model versions
  • Accessible to the provider’s employees
  • Stored in a jurisdiction with different data protection laws than yours

Even when the provider’s policy says they do not use your data for training, your data has still left your plant’s network. For many things this is acceptable. For some things it is a contract violation and a privacy breach.

What never to paste into a public LLM:

  • Customer names, contracts, drawings, or specifications
  • Proprietary process parameters — feeds, speeds, coolant concentrations developed over years
  • Anything export-controlled (in Canada, the Controlled Goods Program; in the US, ITAR/EAR — defense-adjacent manufacturers are commonly affected; when in doubt, ask compliance)
  • Employee personal data
  • Source code with embedded credentials or secrets
  • Drawings or models marked “Confidential” or “Proprietary”
  • Anything you would not send to your competitor’s email address

Real cases:

Case Year What happened
Samsung 2023 Engineers pasted source code into ChatGPT to debug it. Samsung banned internal LLM use company-wide within a month.
Federal court sanctions 2023–2024 Multiple lawyers filed court briefs containing fabricated case citations generated by ChatGPT. Judges imposed monetary sanctions.
Air Canada chatbot 2024 A BC tribunal ruled Air Canada liable for false information given by its customer-service chatbot. Companies are responsible for what their AI tools say.

Safe alternatives:

Need Safe approach
Draft a generic SOP template Abstract your situation; do not paste proprietary procedures
Summarize a long technical document Redact identifying details, or use an enterprise tool with contractual data-protection guarantees
Debug code that touches sensitive systems Strip credentials, names, and paths; reproduce the bug in a minimal isolated example
Engineering work with real customer or defense data Use only your employer’s approved enterprise LLM (Claude Team/Enterprise, ChatGPT Enterprise, Microsoft Copilot for Business), or a locally-hosted model (Ollama, LM Studio)
Letting an agent or connector read your files, email or plant systems Use only approved connectors, give read-only access to the specific folder it needs, and keep production, PLC and customer systems out of reach. Anything an agent can read, it can be tricked into sending elsewhere

9.13.3 Documenting and Disclosing Your AI Use

Professionals keep records of how work was produced, and AI is now part of that record. Two audiences need it: the person who checks your work, and you, six months later, when someone asks how a number was derived.

In this course, AI use is decided assessment by assessment: required, permitted with disclosure, or not permitted (see “Use of generative AI” at the front of this book). Where it is permitted you must be able to explain every line you submit, and where disclosure is required you add a one-line note naming the tool and what you used it for. An assessment that is not listed is not permitted; ask first.

In industry, follow your employer’s policy, and keep a simple AI use log for any work that feeds a design, a safety document or a customer deliverable:

Date Tool and mode Task What I gave it What I changed or rejected How I verified
2026-03-02 Chat, thinking mode Draft PFMEA rows for press cell Process steps only, no drawings Removed 3 invented failure modes, fixed severity ratings Reviewed with the team lead against the process flow

An example disclosure: “I used a chatbot to suggest the structure of this report and to check my Cpk calculation. All figures, data and conclusions are my own, and I verified the calculation by hand.”

Ownership and liability. The legal position on who owns AI-generated text and images is still unsettled and differs by country. The practical rule is simple: the engineer who signs the work is responsible for it, whatever tool produced the first draft.

9.15 Summary

9.15.1 Key Takeaways

  1. The Education Paradox: Strong foundational knowledge is required to get quality AI assistance. “AI will do it for me” is a dangerous mindset.

  2. The Four Steps: Effective AI use follows a consistent process: Define → Prompt → Iterate → Validate. Skipping steps leads to poor results.

  3. Prompt Engineering is a Skill: Like technical writing, crafting effective prompts requires practice and follows learnable principles.

  4. Choose the Right Tool: Different AI tools serve different purposes. Chat interfaces for exploration, NotebookLM for source-grounded research, coding agents for development, and browser extensions for quick assistance (with projects and agents for larger jobs, with limited permissions).

  5. Iteration is Normal: Professional engineers should expect 3-6 turns of AI conversation for complex problems.

  6. Critical Evaluation is Essential: AI produces confident-sounding outputs that may be wrong. Verify everything.

  7. Human Judgment Remains Central: AI augments but does not replace engineering expertise, especially for safety-critical decisions.

  8. Agents Need Boundaries: Give an agent the least permission that works, ask for a plan first, make it verify its work, and treat everything it reads as untrusted.

  9. Protect Confidential Information: Know what must never be pasted into a public tool, and prefer approved enterprise tools.

  10. Document Your AI Use: Keep a record, disclose it where required, and remember that you are responsible for the result.

9.18 Glossary

Prompt Engineering: The practice of designing inputs to AI systems to achieve desired outputs.

Iteration/Multi-turn Conversation: Using multiple exchanges with AI to progressively refine and improve outputs.

Augmentation: Using AI to enhance human capabilities rather than replace them entirely.

Automation: Delegating complete tasks to AI with minimal human intervention.

Hallucination: AI generating plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information.

Context Window: The amount of text an AI can consider at once; important for complex problems requiring extensive background.

Chat Interface: Web-based conversational AI platforms (e.g., Claude.ai, ChatGPT, Gemini) for flexible, general-purpose AI interaction.

NotebookLM: Google’s AI tool that provides source-grounded answers based on uploaded documents, with citation links and audio overview generation.

Claude Code: A command-line AI tool that can read, write, and execute code directly in project environments with full file system access.

Browser Extension: AI tools integrated into web browsers for instant assistance without switching applications; useful for webpage analysis and quick queries.

Source-Grounded AI: AI systems that base responses on specific provided documents rather than general training data, reducing hallucination risk.

Project: A saved AI workspace with standing instructions and reference files that every chat inside it starts from.

Tool Use / Connector: A capability given to an AI model (search, run code, read files, query a system), often through the Model Context Protocol (MCP).

Agent: A language model with tools and a loop: it plans, acts, observes the result and repeats until the goal is met.

Multi-Agent System (“Swarm”): An orchestrator that splits a large job among several agents, each with its own context, often in parallel, sometimes with a reviewer agent.

Reasoning Model: A model that spends extra effort thinking through a problem before answering. Better at multi-step problems, slower and costlier, and still fallible.

Context Engineering: Choosing what a model sees (documents, instructions, examples) so that its context window holds what matters and nothing else.

Prompt Injection: Hidden or embedded text in a document, web page or email that tries to steer an AI agent that reads it.

Grounding: Answering from supplied documents, with quotes, instead of from the model’s memory.

AI Use Log: A short record of what AI was used for, what was provided, what was changed and how the result was verified.
