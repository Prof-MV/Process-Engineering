18.2 Lab 1 — Engineering Design Thinking: Popsicle-Stick Bridge

You work through the full engineering design-thinking process — define the problem, research, generate concepts, analyse feasibility, prototype, test, iterate — by designing and building a popsicle-stick bridge that spans 30 cm and carries the largest possible load without failing. The lab is run in teams and mirrors how a real product moves from a napkin sketch to a tested prototype.

18.2.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Apply the stages of the engineering design process to an open-ended build task.
  • Produce initial and final design sketches and a materials inventory.
  • Carry out a simple failure-mode analysis (FMEA) on your own design and justify the risks you accept.
  • Load-test a structure, record its failure mode, and relate the result to tension, compression and load distribution.
  • Reflect on team performance through a peer evaluation.

18.2.2 Prep work

Complete before the lab period:

  1. Read Lab 1 Supplemental.pdf — overview of bridge types, technical vs. manufacturing/safety design aspects, the 8-stage engineering design process, and design thinking (the “FAIL = first attempt in learning” idea).
  2. Watch the assigned prelab videos on FOL (bridge types; tension/compression).
  3. Form teams of 2–3.
  4. Come with a first rough sketch and a filled-in “Chart 1” (Identify the need & constraints → Research the problem → Develop possible design solutions → Select a promising solution).

18.2.3 Materials & equipment

Provided per team (you may use only provided materials):

  • Small craft sticks — maximum 100
  • Jumbo craft sticks — maximum 20
  • White (PVA) glue
  • Hot-glue gun — sticks joined with hot glue limited to 10
  • Masking tape — maximum 50 cm total
  • String — maximum 100 cm total
  • Ruler / measuring tape
  • Camera or phone (to document the build)

Shared: load-test rig (two supports 30 cm apart), calibrated weights / sandbags, loading hook or basket.

Size constraints: span 30 cm between supports; height ≤ 10 cm; structurally sound and symmetrical.

18.2.4 Procedure

Phase 1 — Problem definition & initial design

  1. Restate the problem: a bridge spanning 30 cm that holds the highest possible load. Populate your thought process using Chart 1.
  2. Sketch your proposed design (beams, trusses, supports) and state the design goals (maximise load capacity, minimise material, efficient structure).
  3. FMEA: list the failure modes your design could have and, for each, give a fix or a rationale for accepting it.
  4. Materials inventory: list every material and quantity you will use.

Phase 2 — Prototyping & building

  1. Build to the approved design. Photograph the build and note construction methods and any changes.
  2. You may prototype individual components (not the whole bridge) to prove design intent. Stay within all material and size limits.

Phase 3 — Testing & evaluation

  1. Place the bridge on the two supports. Add weight at the centre until it fails (breaks or deforms beyond use).
  2. Record the maximum weight held and describe the failure mode.
  3. Compare results across teams; discuss which structural elements helped or hurt, how your design evolved, and what you would change next time.

18.2.5 Deliverables & submission

Due at the start of the next lab class (see FOL). One group report plus individual components:

  1. Design report (group): description of the design process; initial and final sketches; max load held and total bridge mass; discussion of failure modes and structural principles learned; any post-failure modifications and re-test results.
  2. Peer evaluation / reflection (individual, submitted separately, seen only by the professor): what you would do differently, what worked and what did not in your group.
  3. Individual member report requirements as outlined on FOL — done on your own.

Use the template Lab- 1 - Engineering Design Process Flow - Lab Report.docx.

AI tools may not be used for any part of the group or individual report.

18.2.6 Rubric

From Lab 1 - Rubric.docx — 11 criteria, 1 mark each (11 marks, rescaled to the lab’s course weight):

# Criterion Marks
1 Objective & problem definition — clear statement; design targets (safety, scalability, modularity) 1
2 Research & concept generation — research on bridge designs/structural principles; multiple concepts with rationale 1
3 Feasibility analysis — materials, constraints, potential failure modes 1
4 Initial design sketches — clear, detailed; planned materials & quantities documented 1
5 Construction procedure — step-by-step, logical sequence 1
6 Final design sketches & modifications — final sketches; changes after testing and their outcomes 1
7 Failure modes & structural principles — ≥ 3 failure modes identified and explained 1
8 Prototype testing, results & observations — testing documented with photos; max weight and materials recorded 1
9 Peer evaluation & reflection — individual reflection; peer evaluation of teamwork 1
10 Conclusion — concise summary of findings 1
11 Overall presentation — organisation, clarity, adherence to guidelines 1

18.2.7 Instructor notes

  • Prep: cut/verify one class set of load-test rigs (supports exactly 30 cm apart); confirm the calibrated weight set and loading basket; count out material kits per team before class so the 100/20/50 cm/100 cm/10 limits are physically enforced.
  • Safety: hot-glue gun burns are the main hazard — supervise, provide stands and a cool-down area. Eye protection during load-test-to-failure; keep spectators back from the rig; expect sticks to fly on failure.
  • Marking: criteria 1–3 and 7 are where design-thinking (vs. “we just glued sticks”) is demonstrated — weight feedback there. Individual reflections are submitted separately and are confidential to the professor.
  • Time: ~15 min briefing, 20 min design/FMEA sign-off, 60 min build, 30 min testing, 15 min cleanup.

18.3 Lab 2 — Logic Thinking for Robot Control: LightBot & Rocksi

An introduction to programming logic — sequencing, loops and conditionals — and to robot teach-pendant operation, using two free browser simulators: LightBot (a puzzle game that teaches sequence/loop/conditional thinking) and Rocksi (a teach-pendant simulator for the Sawyer robot). The focus is on optimising the number of programming steps.

18.3.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Explain why programming logic matters for reliable, safe, repeatable automation.
  • Use sequences, loops and conditionals to solve a control problem with the fewest steps.
  • Describe the role of a teach pendant (jogging, teaching points, deadman/E-stop, reduced-speed teach mode, I/O testing).
  • Program a simple pick-and-place-style sequence on a simulated teach pendant.

18.3.2 Prep work

  • Read Process Engineering Lab#2 Programming Logic.pdf (importance of programming logic; loops; conditionals; teach pendants).
  • Watch: Rocksi robot simulator: Meet Sawyer.

  • Check that LightBot and Rocksi load on your device.

18.3.3 Materials & equipment

  • Computer or tablet with internet access — one per student (this is an individual lab)
  • Access to LightBot (browser)
  • Access to Rocksi teach-pendant simulator (browser)
  • Lab worksheet (provided — the questions are reproduced in the Procedure below)

18.3.4 Procedure

Part 1 — Programming logic with LightBot

  1. Sequencing. Complete the first levels. Worksheet: describe the sequence of commands you used to complete Level 1. (1 mark)
  2. Loops. Complete levels that require loops. Worksheet: how did using loops let you complete the level more efficiently? (2 marks)
  3. Conditionals. Complete levels that use conditional commands. Worksheet: give an example of a conditional you used and explain its purpose. (2 marks)
  4. Challenge — maximum level & optimisation. Reach Level 15 with the minimum number of steps. Worksheet: describe your strategy and how you optimised. (2 marks)

Part 2 — Robot teach-pendant simulation with Rocksi

  1. Basic movements. Jog the robot to several positions. Worksheet: describe the steps to move the robot to Position A. (1 mark)
  2. Programming a sequence. Program a sequence of moves with the teach pendant. Worksheet: list the command sequence you used. (1 mark)
  3. Simulating inputs & outputs. Simulate an I/O to troubleshoot a robot operation. Worksheet: explain how you simulated the I/O and its effect. (1 mark)

Finish each part with a short reflection on how the skills apply to engineering design and safety.

18.3.5 Deliverables & submission

Completed worksheet (all questions above plus the two reflections), submitted on FOL by the end of the lab period. Assessment is based on task completion in both simulators and the quality of the written answers.

18.3.6 Rubric

From the handout’s marking table — 10 marks total:

Task Marks
Sequencing (LightBot) 1
Loops (LightBot) 2
Conditionals (LightBot) 2
Challenge task (LightBot) — reach Level 15, optimised 2
Basic movements (Rocksi) 1
Programming a sequence (Rocksi) 1
Simulating inputs & outputs (Rocksi) 1

(The handout body lists the LightBot challenge at 2 marks; an earlier summary line totals the same tasks to 10. Use the table above.)

18.3.7 Instructor notes

  • Prep: confirm both simulators are reachable through the lab network/firewall the day before — LightBot and Rocksi are third-party sites and are the single point of failure for this lab. Have an offline fallback (screenshots / a slide walk-through) ready.
  • Individual lab — one machine per student; no group submission.
  • Marking: award loop/conditional marks for explanation, not just level completion. For the challenge task, ask for the step count as evidence of optimisation.

18.4 Lab 3 — Engineering Design Process Documentation

Using the bridge you built in Lab 1, you create the engineering documents that a real project needs to move a design into production: specification sheets, a bill of materials, a task list / process flow, a project schedule and Gantt chart, a production-facility layout drawing, and a health-safety-environment (HSE) risk assessment. The tools are Microsoft Visio/Word, Project and Excel.

18.4.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Explain terms such as HSE, Basic Engineering Design Data (BEDD), Front-End Engineering Design (FEED), fast-tracking, project schedule and General Arrangement (GA) drawing.
  • Produce a specification sheet and a bill of materials for a simple product.
  • Build a task list / process-flow chart with durations and turn it into a project schedule and Gantt chart in Microsoft Project.
  • Draft a production-facility layout drawing showing product in/out, stations, material and people flow.
  • Complete an HSE risk-assessment for a build process.

18.4.2 Prep work

  • Review Unit 2 content and Lab 3 - Supplemental.pdf (the “Lab-2 connection”, the detail-design/engineering process-flow diagram, and example templates).
  • Watch: 3DEXPERIENCE Delmia — automated bridge manufacturing factory (link in the supplement).
  • Bring your Lab 1 bridge, sketches and photos.
  • Confirm you can open Microsoft Visio/Word, Project and Excel on a lab PC.

18.4.3 Materials & equipment

  • Lab PC with Microsoft Word/Visio, Microsoft Project, Microsoft Excel
  • Templates from FOL:
    • Bill_of_Materials_Template_for_Excel.xlsx
    • Gantt_chart.mpp (Microsoft Project example file)
    • HSE risk-assessment Word template (Risk assessment template)
  • Your Lab 1 bridge, sketches and build photos

18.4.4 Procedure

Phase 1 — Specification sheets & bill of materials. Examine the materials in your bridge and create specification sheets for the craft sticks and other materials, following the BOM examples in the supplement. Fill in the Excel BOM template.

Phase 2 — Project schedule. Build a chronological task list (flow chart) for making the bridge; add a column for the time each task needs; open the example .mpp file, enter the tasks and durations, and generate a schedule and Gantt chart. Use the task-numbering scheme (Task 1 → Sub-task 11 → Sub-sub-task 121…) from the supplement.

Phase 3 — Production-facility layout drawing. From the task list, allocate spaces in a hypothetical plant to perform those tasks. Sketch a first layout, discuss with your team and the instructor, then finalise it. Show product in / product out, stations, tool and material locations, and raw/waste/people flow.

Phase 4 — Safety documentation (HSE report). Identify at least three health-and-safety risks in the bridge-building process and document them in the risk-assessment template (hazard, who is harmed and how, current controls, further action, owner, deadline, done).

18.4.5 Deliverables & submission

The lab report is the set of documents from all four phases: specification sheets + BOM, task list/flow chart, Project schedule + Gantt chart, facility layout drawing, and the completed HSE report. Submit on FOL by the end of the lab period.

18.6 Lab 5 — Lean Manufacturing Simulation

A hands-on paper-cutting production line. Two teams assemble four paper “triangle products” (house, tent, tree, umbrella) over three timed trials, changing the rules each trial to expose muda (waste). You measure time, count opportunities and defects, compute a defect ratio, and identify improvements — the core of a lean kaizen loop.

18.6.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Identify value-adding vs. wasteful steps in a simple production line.
  • Assign line roles (supervisor, supplies handler, assemblers, quality inspector) and run to a standard.
  • Measure cycle time, count opportunities and defects, and compute defects per opportunity.
  • Propose and test process improvements across successive trials, trading speed against accuracy.

18.6.2 Prep work

  • Review Unit 4 content and the Lab 4 / lean supplementary documents.
  • Have a checklist and a timer ready.
  • Read the product check sheet and product images so you know exactly what each of the eight products must contain.

18.6.3 Materials & equipment

Per session (two teams of ~5):

  • A4 paper — pink
  • A4 paper — yellow
  • Blue pens and red pens
  • Scissors (several per team)
  • Stopwatch / timer per team
  • Printed product check sheet, product images, and order tickets

18.6.4 Procedure

Assign roles: line supervisor, supplies handler, assemblers (as many as needed), quality-control inspector.

Prep the line: (1) cut A4 sheets in two; (2) make three squares from one piece and cut each in half to get triangles; (3) cut triangles into smaller triangles. Group 1 makes Yellow House, Pink Tent, Yellow Tree, Pink Umbrella; Group 2 makes Pink House, Yellow Tent, Pink Tree, Yellow Umbrella. “Opportunities” = the number of drawn objects on each product (see the images).

Trial 1 rules: time the assembly of all four products; the supervisor may touch only the finished product and is the only one who moves product to inventory; every product must travel with the QC checklist.

Trial 2 rules: supervisor still only touches the finished product; only the supplies handler may fetch materials for assemblers; every product must travel with an order ticket; the supplies handler has a reference image; switch product lines.

Trial 3 rules: switch product lines again; same handler/supervisor rules; the supervisor must cut at least two workers (budget cut) and may re-assign roles to streamline further.

Each trial, record: time for four products; number of opportunities; number of defects; ratio of defects to total opportunities; and a list of wasteful steps / problems with proposed improvements.

18.6.5 Deliverables & submission

Group worksheet with the three-trial data table (time, opportunities, defects, defect ratio) and the improvement notes for each trial, plus a short conclusion on what changed between trials and why. Submit on FOL by the end of the lab period.

18.6.6 Rubric

TODO — no numeric rubric in the folder. Suggested split (confirm before the term):

Element Marks

Trial 2 data complete + waste analysis 3

18.6.7 Instructor notes

  • Prep to buy each term: 1 ream pink A4, 1 ream yellow A4, 1 box blue pens, 1 box red pens. Print: product check sheets, product image sheets, order tickets, QC checklists — one set per team plus spares.
  • Prep to stage: class set of scissors; two stopwatches (or phone timers).
  • Run with exactly two teams of ~5; you need room for two parallel lines with a supervisor “inventory” area each.
  • Marking: reward quantified waste analysis (specific wasted motion / wait / rework) and evidence the team’s changes between trials were driven by the data.

18.7 Lab 6 — Ergonomics in Automated Manufacturing (REBA & Inseer)

You assess three workplace tasks for musculoskeletal risk two ways: manually with the REBA (Rapid Entire Body Assessment) worksheet, and automatically with Inseer, an AI video-based ergonomic-assessment tool. You then compare the two methods.

18.7.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Explain why ergonomics matters in an automated manufacturing facility and name common ergonomic risks and their effects on workers.
  • Score a task with the REBA worksheet and interpret the result.
  • Generate an ergonomic risk-assessment report with Inseer.
  • Compare manual vs. automated assessment — advantages and limitations of each.

18.7.2 Prep work

  • Review the introduction-to-ergonomics and REBA material (video links in the handout, Procedure topics 1–2).
  • Skim Cheatsheet - Egonomics -REBA.pdf and the REBA-worksheet.pdf.
  • Form groups of 3–4.

18.7.3 Materials & equipment

  • REBA Employee Assessment Worksheet (printed, one per student/group)
  • Inseer software tool — web-based; account/login required
  • Computer with internet access
  • Sample tasks from an automated manufacturing facility (videos / photo sets):
    • Task 1 — lifting boxes repeatedly
    • Task 2 — computer-workstation setup
    • Task 3 — floor cleaning with a buffer

18.7.4 Procedure

  1. Intro to ergonomics — watch the overview videos (importance; common risks and impacts).
  2. Overview of REBA — watch how to use the worksheet.
  3. Task evaluation — each group is assigned one of the three tasks. Observe and analyse it, note ergonomic risks (repetitive motion, awkward posture, heavy lifting, vibration, glare, chair height …), score it with the REBA worksheet, and interpret the score.
  4. Intro to Inseer — overview of the tool; input the task data and generate a risk-assessment report.
  5. Data & observations — record REBA scores and Inseer results for the task; note insights.
  6. Analysis — compare the manual REBA assessment with the Inseer report; discuss advantages and limitations of each.
  7. Conclusion — reflect on the importance of ergonomics in automated manufacturing and the benefits of AI tools like Inseer.

18.7.5 Deliverables & submission

One group report containing: the procedure, your group’s REBA analysis, your group’s Inseer report, and your group’s observations and conclusions. Submit on FOL by the end of the lab period.

18.8 Lab 7 — Safety in Automated Manufacturing, Part 1

An introduction to industrial-robot anatomy and safeguarding: the four robot components (end-effector, manipulator, controller, teach pendant), collaborative vs. non-collaborative robots, the range of safeguarding devices, and the robot application spaces (maximum, restricted, operating envelope).

18.8.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Identify the four main components of an industrial robot and the function of each.
  • Distinguish collaborative from non-collaborative robot applications.
  • Describe the common safeguarding devices — fixed/perimeter guards, interlocked barrier guards, light curtains, pressure-sensitive mats, laser scanners, speed-and-separation monitoring, safety-rated monitored stop, hand-guided control, power-and-force limiting.
  • Explain and sketch the maximum, restricted and operating space envelopes for a robot arm.

18.11 Lab 10 — Safety Instrumented System Architectures

An introduction to the architectures used in Safety Instrumented Systems (SIS) — why a safety integrity level (SIL) is not just a probability-of-failure calculation, and how architectural constraints (hardware fault tolerance, safe failure fraction) shape the structure. You work through the MooN family of architectures: 1oo1, 1oo1D, 1oo2, 1oo2D and 2oo2, comparing their dependability.

18.11.1 Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Define system, subsystem, element, component, channel and group as used in IEC 61508.
  • Explain the three constraint types on a safety instrumented function — systematic capability, architectural, and probabilistic performance — and the parameters SFF, HFT and PFD.
  • Describe the 1oo1, 1oo1D, 1oo2, 1oo2D and 2oo2 architectures and their effect on safe vs. dangerous failure behaviour.
  • Select an architecture for a stated SIL / fault-tolerance requirement and justify it.
