19  Labs

This chapter is a catalogue of labs. It holds more labs than any one term can deliver: an instructor picks the labs that suit their course, their own background and the equipment available. Labs are identified by name, not by number — no lab depends on where it sits in this chapter. Where labs do depend on one another, the catalogue below and each lab’s Prep work say so.

The chapter is organised by topic. Each topic is a sub-chapter, and each lab is a section within its topic. The last sub-chapter, Teacher preparation, is a planning aid for the instructor: which labs must be delivered together, how to schedule the materials-testing rotation, and the room, equipment, software and shopping lists for every lab.

19.1 Robotics and Programming Logic

Two labs that bookend a course: block-based programming logic on a browser robot simulator, and the design and programming of a work cell for a real 6-axis industrial arm in RoboDK. Neither needs an install. They pair with Chapter 8 (Industrial Robotics).

19.1.1 Logic Thinking for Robot Control: Rocksi

An introduction to programming logic — sequencing, loops and conditional/branching logic — and to programming a robot arm, using one free browser simulator: Rocksi (a browser, block-based simulator for a collaborative robot arm — Scratch-style blocks, no install and no login). You build the logic concepts and a pick-and-place sequence in the same tool, and the focus is on optimising the number of blocks.

(Earlier versions of this lab also used LightBot; it needs a Flash-era plug-in that is not available on college machines, so the whole lab now runs in Rocksi.)

AI use: not permitted — the outcome is your own logic. The Robotic Work-Cell Design lab returns to a real 6-axis arm in RoboDK for Web.

Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Explain why programming logic matters for reliable, safe, repeatable automation.
  • Use sequences, loops and conditional/branching blocks to solve a control problem with the fewest blocks.
  • Describe the role of a teach pendant (jogging, teaching points, deadman/E-stop, reduced-speed teach mode, I/O testing).
  • Program a simple pick-and-place-style sequence on a block-based robot simulator and simulate a digital input/output.

Prep work

  • Read the Programming Logic handout (importance of programming logic; loops; conditionals; teach pendants).
  • Watch: Rocksi robot simulator: Meet Sawyer.

  • Open Rocksi and confirm it loads and runs on your device. Try dragging one move block onto the workspace and pressing run.

Materials & equipment

  • Computer with internet access — one per student (all labs are individual)
  • Access to Rocksi block-based robot-arm simulator (browser, free, no login, no plug-in)
  • The Snipping Tool (Windows) for screenshots you will upload
  • The FOL quiz for this lab — it carries every task, in order, and is where you type your answers and upload your Rocksi program and screenshots
NoteInstructions & submission

Work through the FOL quiz for this lab during the lab period. It covers sequencing, loops, conditional/branching logic, a pick-and-place sequence, simulating a digital I/O, and an optimisation challenge; for each task you describe what you did and attach a screenshot, and at the end you upload your saved Rocksi program.

19.1.2 Robotic Work-Cell Design in RoboDK for Web

A take-home lab: released about three weeks before it is due, done individually, with a short screen recording as part of the submission. You design and program the Précitech kitting cell’s pick-and-place robot in RoboDK for Web — a free, no-install, in-browser robot simulator with 600+ real arms. It bookends Logic Thinking: block-logic puzzles at the start, a real 6-axis industrial arm at the end. You close the loop on the course by checking the robot’s cycle time against the takt time you computed in the Lean lab.

AI use: permitted with disclosure. The station layout, the program and the cycle times must be your own work.

Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Jog a 6-axis robot in joint, linear and tool coordinates and recognise singularities and joint-limit stops.
  • Lay out a work cell and verify that every pick and place point lies inside the robot’s reach envelope.
  • Program a pick-and-place cycle with targets and gripper I/O and read the simulated cycle time.
  • Compare a simulated cycle time against a required takt time and optimise the program to close the gap.
  • Compute a minimum safeguarding distance from robot stopping performance and select an appropriate safeguard.

Prep work

  • Read Chapter 8 (industrial robotics — coordinate systems, the work envelope, singularities) and Chapter 6 (machine guarding — the minimum-distance / safeguarding-distance formula and the safeguard hierarchy).
  • Have your takt calculation from the Lean lab to hand — you need the takt time for 250 kits/day.
  • Open RoboDK for Web and confirm it loads and runs a sample station in your browser. No account or licence key is required.
  • Confirm your screen-recording tool works (the same tool the course plan’s take-home lab uses).

Materials & equipment

  • Personal computer with internet access and a browser (this is a take-home lab — no lab PC required)
  • RoboDK for Web — no install, no licence key
  • A screen-recording tool — the built-in recorder is enough (Windows Xbox Game Bar, Win+G; macOS Screenshot, Shift+Cmd+5) or free OBS Studio
  • Your takt time from the Lean lab; the Précitech case study (250 kits/day, one 8-hour shift)
  • The FOL quiz for this lab — the five tasks in order, and where you upload the report, the RoboDK station file and the 3–5 min screen recording

19.2 Process Design and Simulation

The engineering documentation a new production cell needs before it is built, and a discrete-event simulation of that cell — both set at Précitech Components. The simulation lab builds on the documentation lab, so deliver them in that order. They pair with Chapters 2, 4 and 16.

19.2.1 Engineering Design Process Documentation

You produce the engineering-documentation package that a real project needs to move a design into production — specification sheets, a bill of materials, a task list / process flow, a project schedule and Gantt chart, a production-facility layout drawing, and a health-safety-environment (HSE) risk assessment — for the Précitech Components shaft-kit assembly cell described in the case study below. The same cell is simulated in Facility Design & Simulation, so the documents you build here feed directly into the next lab. Tools: Microsoft Visio/Word, Project and Excel.

AI use: not permitted for any part of the report.

NoteCase study — Précitech Components: new shaft-kit assembly & packing cell

Précitech Components is a Tier-1 automotive supplier in Granby, Québec. Its CNC turning cells produce transmission shaft blanks (AISI 4140 steel, Ø 25.000 mm ± 0.050 mm) for a major OEM customer. The OEM now wants the shafts delivered as a ready-to-install kit rather than as loose blanks, so Précitech is adding a manual kitting, sub-assembly and packing cell immediately downstream of the CNC turning cells.

One shaft kit contains: 1 machined shaft blank, 2 tapered roller bearings, 1 retaining ring, 1 lip seal, 1 dowel pin, 1 desiccant sachet — placed in a moulded tray inside a labelled carton. The exact part numbers, quantities and shaft dimensions are on the assembly drawing (410-2365, Rev B) and shaft detail drawing (205-5478, Rev B) — you need both for this lab.

Target output: 250 kits/day on one 8-hour shift.

Process outline: finished blanks arrive from the CNC cells on a trolley; bought-in components (bearings, rings, seals, pins) are held in a small storage rack that is replenished from the main warehouse when stock falls to a reorder level; an operator picks the components for one kit, presses the two bearings onto the shaft, fits the retaining ring and lip seal, inspects the assembly against a checklist, places it in a tray, closes and labels the carton, and stages it for the shipping dock.

Plant manager Sophie Tran has asked you to produce the engineering documentation package for this cell before it is built.

This is the same plant used in the machine-learning labs (see Machine Learning), which build a predictive-maintenance proof of concept for Précitech’s CNC turning cells.

Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

  • Explain terms such as HSE, Basic Engineering Design Data (BEDD), Front-End Engineering Design (FEED), fast-tracking, project schedule and General Arrangement (GA) drawing.
  • Produce a specification sheet and a bill of materials for a manufactured product.
  • Build a task list / process-flow chart with durations and turn it into a project schedule and Gantt chart in Microsoft Project.
  • Draft a production-facility layout drawing showing product in/out, stations, storage, and material and people flow, and mark starvation / blockage risk points.
  • Compare a highly automated (turntable) cell against a manual U-cell and justify a configuration choice for a given volume, timeline and labour situation.
  • Complete an HSE risk assessment for a manual assembly cell.

Prep work

  • Read the case study above and Unit 2 content on the basics of process engineering and design.
  • Review the assembly drawing (410-2365, Rev B) and the shaft detail drawing (205-5478, Rev B) — you need the part numbers, quantities, materials and key dimensions from these for your spec sheet and BOM. Purchased components are sourced from McMaster-Carr.
  • Skim the Design Documentation supplement for the document templates and the detail-design / engineering process-flow diagram (ignore the popsicle-bridge framing — the document types are the same).
  • Confirm you can open Microsoft Visio/Word, Project and Excel on a lab PC.

Materials & equipment

  • Lab PC with Microsoft Word / Visio, Microsoft Project, Microsoft Excel
  • Assembly drawing 410-2365, Rev B and shaft drawing 205-5478, Rev B — the part numbers, quantities and dimensions you need
  • Templates from FOL:
    • Bill_of_Materials_Template_for_Excel.xlsx — pre-filled with the kit’s part numbers, descriptions and quantities from the assembly drawing; you fill in unit, source and supplier
    • Gantt_chart_template.xlsx — open it in Microsoft Project (File > Open) and use Project’s Import Wizard to turn it into a schedule, then Save As .mpp; the template’s “Read me first” sheet walks you through it
    • Risk_Assessment_Template.docx (HSE risk assessment)
  • The Précitech shaft-kit case study (above) — the single source of requirements
  • The FOL quiz for this lab — every phase in order, and where you upload your spec sheets, BOM .xlsx, flow chart, Project .mpp, layout drawing and risk assessment
NoteThe automation decision

Précitech’s plant manager has not committed to a cell configuration yet — that choice is yours to make and defend as part of your facility layout. Two realistic options are on the table for a 250-kit/day, single-shift cell:

Option A — highly automated: rotary turntable, 6–7 stations. An indexing turntable carries the shaft through fixed stations (press bearing 1, press bearing 2, fit retaining ring, fit seal, insert dowel pin + desiccant, inspect, place in tray / close carton), with one operator at a single load/unload station. Everything else is tooled and sensored.

Option B — manual U-cell. A standard U-shaped layout (Chapter 4) with one operator per station, parts and trays moving between stations on roller conveyors — matching the manual kitting cell Précitech originally scoped.

Option A — automated turntable Option B — manual U-cell
Capital cost High — custom turntable + tooled/sensored stations Low — conveyor + hand tools/fixtures
Time to stand up Long — design, build and debug before day 1 Short — matches the “before it is built” schedule pressure
Labour 1 operator/shift Up to 6–7 operators/shift
Cycle-time consistency High — paced by the index, not by people Lower — depends on operator skill and fatigue
Flexibility to rebalance Low — station count and index time are fixed High — move people, cross-train, absorb absences
Changeover to a kit variant Retool multiple stations at once Operators adapt; fixtures swap faster
Failure mode Single point of failure — one jammed station stops the whole cell A blocked station can sometimes be buffered around
Ergonomic risk Concentrated at one manual station Spread across every station (Ergonomics)
Maintenance skill needed PLC/servo/automation technicians (Chapter 9) Standard mechanical/tooling upkeep

Neither option is “correct.” Your facility-layout task in the FOL quiz asks you to pick one, sketch it, and justify the choice with at least two pros and two cons — from the table above or your own reasoning — tied to Précitech’s actual volume, timeline and labour situation.

19.5.3 Taper Angle Measurement with a Sine Bar

A sine bar turns an angle measurement into a length measurement. The bar, a stack of gauge blocks and the surface plate form a right-angled triangle: the bar is the hypotenuse L and the gauge-block stack is the side h opposite the angle, so \(\sin\theta = h/L\). You build a gauge-block stack under one roller of the sine bar until the top surface of an unknown taper is parallel to the surface plate — confirmed by sliding a dial indicator along it and reading zero deviation — and calculate the taper angle. You measure three tapers.

19.6.5 Repeatability and Reproducibility (Gauge R&R)

A measurement system has two sources of variation you can study directly: repeatability (one person measuring the same item repeatedly) and reproducibility (different people measuring the same item). Here the “instrument” is a caliper, and calipers differ too, so a difference between two people might be the people or might be their calipers. This lab pulls the two apart. You have your own numbered caliper and measure the diameter and the length of ten numbered steel bars that were sawn to length, twice each, in a randomised order. Then you swap calipers with a partner and measure all ten bars twice more with their caliper. Afterwards you and your partner exchange readings, so each of you holds a complete 2 operators × 2 calipers study.

Before you measure anything you check both calipers against a gauge block and record each one’s bias. You then work out, for each characteristic, repeatability (EV), the operator effect (AV), the caliper effect (CV), R&R, part variation, total variation, %GRR, %GRR of the tolerance and the number of distinct categories, and compare the two characteristics: is the same caliper equally good at measuring a diameter and a length? This is the hands-on, physical version of the study you can also run in the browser in SPC and Gauge R&R: the Virtual Caliper.

The lab is individual: you take your own readings and write your own report. The swap partner only lends you a caliper and, at the end, a copy of their readings.

AI use: not permitted — the measurements and calculations must be your own.

Learning outcomes

After this lab you should be able to:

19.8 Virtual Welding

19.11.4.15 Repeatability and Reproducibility (Gauge R&R)

  • Design: an individual lab built on a swap pair. Each student is an operator with their own serial-numbered caliper. Two students swap calipers, so the study is fully crossed: 2 operators × 2 calipers × 10 bars × 2 trials, for the diameter and the length. Without the swap each operator is confounded with their caliper (the operator and caliper effects cannot be told apart); with it, the operator difference is read with the caliper held constant and the caliper difference with the operator held constant. Each student takes 80 readings (10 bars × 2 trials × 2 calipers × 2 characteristics) and copies their partner’s 80 into their own sheet, so the only thing shared is the readings — the calculations, graphs and answers are the student’s own.
  • Prep — equipment: one caliper per student, each with its own serial number (the sheet records it); one certified gauge block (choose a size near the bar diameter if you have a choice); ten numbered bars per pair, drawn from your pool of 10–30. With 30 bars, three pairs can measure at once; with fewer, stagger the pairs so the same bars are never needed twice at the same moment. Give partners the same ten bars, entered in the same (ascending) order on both sheets, or the rows will not line up. Stamp or engrave the numbers — labels that fall off ruin the study.
  • Prep — pairs and numbers: assign swap partners, and give every student a student number from 1 to 30, different from their partner’s. The number selects the student’s random run order on the sheet (a look-up in the Order table sheet), so students beside each other are rarely after the same bar. With an odd class, let the extra student pair with someone from a finished pair, who then repeats only the swap half of the study using the extra student’s caliper.
  • Prep — workbook: run python R/gen_grr_sheet.py and stage labs/templates/Gauge_RR_Bar_Stock_Data_Sheet.xlsx on FOL. Replace the placeholder tolerances on the Setup sheet (Ø1.000 ± 0.005 in and length 2.000 ± 0.020 in) with the real specification for your bars; %GRR of tolerance depends on them. The sheet is in inches and assumes a 0.0005 in caliper resolution (typical of a digital caliper) — if yours read 0.001 in (dial or vernier), change the resolution cell; it sets the caliper-check limit (two resolution steps). A metric caliper needs the readings and tolerances entered in the same unit throughout. The run orders are fixed random sequences; change the seed in run_orders() to regenerate them.
  • Operational definitions — agree these before the students start: diameter at the mid-length of the bar, jaws square to the axis, one reading, no rotating the bar between trials; length between the two sawn faces, with the jaws placed at the centre of each face (sawn faces are rough and not perfectly square, which is a real source of variation). Students measure both dimensions each time they pick a bar up, in the run order, and must not look at their earlier readings or their partner’s until both have finished.
  • Gauge block: one block means a queue — partners check their two calipers together at the start of the period, one at a time. Clean the jaws and the block, handle the block by its edges, return it to its case. The check covers one size only; use it to discuss what it does not tell you (linearity, the bar’s length).
  • The method and its limits: repeatability EV comes from the average range of the four conditions; the operator effect AV and caliper effect CV each come from the difference between the two levels (d₂* = 1.41), corrected for repeat scatter over the 40 readings behind each average. Two levels each is a crude basis — a small real difference is magnified by the 5.15/1.41 factor — so treat AV and CV as estimates. If you want firmer numbers, pool the class: collect every student’s readings and run a class-level study with many operators and calipers (or an ANOVA, as in Chapter 11). The sheet also reports an operator-by-caliper interaction contrast against about 2σ of repeat scatter: if it is noticeable, the operator effect depends on the caliper and the split is only approximate.
  • Timing (indicative — not tested): caliper check about 10 minutes for the two calipers; 80 readings per student take roughly 45–60 minutes including the swap. Plan a second period for the calculations if the first is short.
  • Answer key (simulated): Gauge RR Bar Stock - EXAMPLE (instructor).xlsx in LabInstructions/Quality_Labs/fixed/ holds simulated readings (not measurements), in inches, with tolerances ± 0.005 in and ± 0.020 in, a 1.000 in gauge block, a caliper bias of 0 and −0.0007 in, and a partner technique offset of +0.0015 in on length. Its formulas were checked against an independent calculation. Diameter: EV ≈ 0.0021, AV ≈ 0.0006, CV ≈ 0.0029, GRR ≈ 0.0036, PV ≈ 0.0043, TV ≈ 0.0056 in, %GRR ≈ 65 %, %tolerance ≈ 36 %, ndc = 1, largest component the caliper. Length: EV ≈ 0.0059, AV ≈ 0.0050, CV ≈ 0.0025, GRR ≈ 0.0082, PV ≈ 0.0541, TV ≈ 0.0547 in, %GRR ≈ 14.9 %, %tolerance ≈ 20 %, ndc = 9. The caliper difference recovered from the bars (+0.0008 in on diameter, +0.0007 in on length) matches the gauge-block difference (+0.0007 in); the operator difference on length (−0.0014 in) matches the built-in offset. Real student results will differ.
  • What to expect (a teaching expectation, not a measured result): sawn lengths vary a lot from bar to bar, while drawn bar stock has almost the same diameter along its stock; so the part variation is large for length and small for diameter. The same caliper therefore tends to look better on length than on diameter when judged against total variation — a good moment to compare %GRR of total variation with %GRR of tolerance, and to ask what the 0.0005 in resolution has to do with it.
  • Constants (5.15σ convention): EV = 5.15 R̄ / 1.128 (d₂, 2 trials); AV = √[(X̄diff,operator × 5.15 / 1.41)² − EV² / 40]; CV = √[(X̄diff,caliper × 5.15 / 1.41)² − EV² / 40], with d₂* = 1.41 for two levels and 40 = 10 bars × 2 trials × 2; PV = 5.15 Rp / 3.18 (d₂* for 10 parts); GRR = √(EV² + AV² + CV²). Ratios such as %GRR do not depend on the convention.
  • Marking: the check of both calipers and their biases, the balanced data set (80 readings per characteristic, 40 of them the partner’s) and how independence was kept, each calculation step (accept answers that follow from the student’s own R̄ and X̄diff), the verdicts against the acceptance bands, and the operator-or-caliper conclusion against the gauge-block difference. Common slips: readings typed in the wrong bar’s row, or the partner’s columns pasted into the wrong place (their C:F go into the student’s G:J); partners using different bars or a different order; results quoted as %GRR of tolerance when total variation was asked (or the reverse); a negative value under the AV or CV root not set to zero; ndc rounded instead of truncated; placeholder tolerances left in; readings in the wrong unit.
